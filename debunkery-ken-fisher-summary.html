Fisher's 7 Debunkery Tactics Apply these to any investing belief you encounter

📐

Scale It

Big numbers are always scary. Put them in proper context — as % of GDP, per share, relative to history.

🔄

Be Counterintuitive

Markets are inherently counterintuitive. If common sense says X, check if the market rewards anti-X.

📜

Check History

"XYZ happened and that's bad." Has XYZ historically led to bad outcomes? Check the data — it's usually free.

🌍

Think Globally

Think outside your country. Many misconceptions dissolve instantly when you consider the full world picture.

💻

Check Data Yourself

Free historical data exists for almost everything. Don't take anyone's word — verify it with Excel correlations.

📦

Use the Four-Box

Check all four possible outcome combinations. Example: Down January + Down Year, Down Jan + Up Year, etc.

❓

Question Consensus

If EVERYONE believes something is true — question it. Flip it on its head and test whether the reverse holds.

The Core Premise

Investing is a probabilities game, not a certainties game. Even the best investors are wrong 30% of the time. The goal isn't zero mistakes — it's reducing your error rate to beat the majority of investors who are wrong more than right.

Why We Get It Wrong

Our Stone Age brains evolved for physical survival, not capital markets. Markets are inherently counterintuitive. We see heightened risk when risk is actually least, seek patterns where none exist, and are blinded by cognitive biases like hindsight, confirmation bias, and myopic loss aversion.

The Power of Capitalism

Stocks rise more than they fall because human ingenuity is boundless. Future earnings from currently unfathomed products and services will always be created. Capitalism has proven more powerful than any anti-capitalistic force in democratic nations. Bet on it.

20 Core Investment Principles Distilled from all 50 bunks — the investing truths Fisher returns to again and again

  • 1

    Investing is a probabilities game, not a certainties game

    No investor is right more than ~70% of the time. The goal is not perfection — it's reducing your error rate. Accept that you will make mistakes, and aim to make fewer of them.

  • 2

    Your time horizon is likely much longer than you think

    A 65-year-old today has a median life expectancy of 85+. Your investing horizon ends when your money runs out, not when you retire. Think in decades, not years.

  • 3

    Stocks are safer than bonds over long periods

    Given 20+ years, stocks beat bonds in 97% of historical periods by a 3.7-to-1 margin. "Safe" bonds subject you to inflation risk, reinvestment risk, and opportunity cost — all of which can be devastating over time.

  • 4

    Normal returns are extreme, not average

    Stocks are up big (above 20%) or down in roughly 68% of years. Years returning "average" returns (10-12%) have occurred only 5 times since 1926. Anyone promising smooth, consistent returns is lying or stealing.

  • 5

    Bull markets are longer and stronger than you remember

    Bull markets average 21% annually and last far longer than bear markets. After big bear markets come big bull bounces. Never change your long-term strategy based on a bear market.

  • 6

    Clarity is the most expensive thing in markets

    New bull markets begin in V-shaped bounces. The first 3 months average 21.8% gains. Waiting for "clear" signals means missing most of the recovery. There is never an "all-clear" signal.

  • 7

    Your emotions are your biggest enemy

    Investors feel the pain of loss 2.5× more intensely than equal gains feel good (myopic loss aversion). This causes panic selling at lows and greed buying at highs. Your Stone Age brain makes a terrible investment advisor.

  • 8

    No investment style is permanently superior

    Growth, value, small-cap, large-cap — all trade leadership irregularly over multi-year periods. Supply and demand (via investment bankers) ultimately equalizes long-term returns across all categories. Never fall permanently in love with any single style.

  • 9

    Separate custody from decision-making — always

    Every major financial fraud in history involved the advisor having custody of client assets. Always hold your money at an independent third-party custodian. This simple step makes Ponzi schemes nearly impossible.

  • 10

    Stop-losses, DCA, covered calls, and annuities mostly enrich your broker

    These products are sold as risk-reducers but don't reliably work that way. Stop-losses are coin flips. DCA works just 31% of the time vs. lump-sum. Annuities carry fees that compound against you for decades.

  • 11

    Passive investing is tactically easy but psychologically nearly impossible

    DALBAR data: equity fund investors averaged 3.2% annually vs. 8.2% for the S&P 500 over 20 years — a 5% gap — because they can't hold for the necessary time. The average fund holding period is just 3.2 years.

  • 12

    Coincident indicators are useless for forecasting

    The VIX, consumer confidence, P/E ratios — these tell you what already happened, not what comes next. A signal you can only identify in hindsight is not a signal. Markets efficiently price in all widely known information.

  • 13

    No single calendar, seasonal, or political rule consistently works

    "So goes January," "Sell in May," dollar direction, Fed rate direction — none of these reliably predict stock market outcomes. The market is too complex for any single indicator to be failsafe.

  • 14

    Stocks lead the economy; never the other way around

    Stocks move 6-12 months before the economy confirms direction. GDP, unemployment, and consumer confidence are lagging or coincident indicators. Waiting for economic confirmation guarantees you miss the move.

  • 15

    Inflation is the silent killer of conservative portfolios

    At 3% average inflation, $25,000 of annual spending needs $60,000 in 30 years to maintain purchasing power. Healthcare inflation runs far above average. "Conservative" investing that doesn't outpace inflation is not safe — it's a slow death sentence for purchasing power.

  • 16

    Think globally; the world is more correlated than you think

    The US is ~25% of global GDP. US and non-US stocks have been highly correlated for centuries and trend together long-term. A truly global portfolio reduces single-country risk and expands your opportunity set. Aim for ~50% non-US exposure.

  • 17

    Budget deficits, high debt, trade deficits, and weak dollars are not stock killers

    Each of these "scary" factors has no reliable correlation with poor stock returns when checked historically. After deficit peaks, stocks average 22% over the next 12 months. Trade deficits reflect economic strength. Stocks rise in weak-dollar and strong-dollar environments equally.

  • 18

    Gold, oil, GDP, and confidence indicators don't predict stock direction

    Correlation between oil and stocks since 1980: -0.02 (essentially zero). Consumer confidence is coincident (reflects what just happened). Gold lost money for 23 consecutive years (1982-2005). GDP is a lagging measure.

  • 19

    The presidential term cycle is real and has fundamental drivers

    Year 3 of any presidency has averaged 17.5% returns — no negative year since 1939. New presidents moderate in their first year, resolving election-year fears (for new Democrats) or disappointments (for new Republicans). This is not voodoo; it has real economic logic.

  • 20

    Concentrate to build wealth; diversify to protect it — but know which road you're on

    Founder-CEOs and senior executives rightfully concentrate. But for the majority of investors on the "save and invest" road, no single stock should exceed 5% of your portfolio — especially not your employer's stock, which doubles your risk.

Investor Action Plan Concrete steps every investor should take, drawn directly from Fisher's 50 bunks

0 of 20 completed
  • Determine your true time horizonCount from today until the last day your portfolio needs to last — likely until the surviving spouse dies, not until retirement. For most people this is 25-40+ years.
  • Build your benchmark (not based on age alone)Determine: (1) time horizon, (2) return expectations, (3) cash flow needs. These three factors — not age — should determine your stock/bond mix.
  • Verify your advisor uses a separate custodianYour investment decisions and your money custody must be at different entities. If your advisor holds your assets directly, investigate or leave. This eliminates Ponzi scheme risk.
  • Check for the 5 fraud warning signsAdvisor has custody · Consistently smooth returns · Murky strategy · Flashy distractions · You never did real due diligence. Any 3+ = investigate immediately.
  • Stop using the Dow as your benchmarkSwitch to market-cap-weighted indices: S&P 500 (US), MSCI World (global), MSCI ACWI (all-country). The Dow is price-weighted and broken. Never reference it for long-term performance.
  • Eliminate or review any stop-loss ordersStock prices are non-serially correlated — past prices don't predict future prices. Stop-losses only guarantee higher transaction costs. A triggered stop is a coin flip on future direction.
  • Audit your variable annuitiesCheck annual fees (typically 2.4% vs 1.2% for mutual funds). Calculate the 20-year fee drag on your portfolio. Factor in surrender periods. Ask: would I be better off in the underlying funds directly?
  • Invest lump sums immediately when availableLump-sum investing beats dollar cost averaging 69% of the time historically. Markets rise more than they fall, so time invested matters more than timing.
  • Calculate your inflation-adjusted cash flow needsAt 3% inflation, $25K/year spending becomes $45K in 20 years, $60K in 30 years. Plan for healthcare inflation running at 2-3× the general CPI. "Conservative" portfolios that don't outpace inflation are not safe.
  • Ensure your portfolio has meaningful non-US exposureTarget ~40-50% non-US equity allocation. US stocks are ~49% of world equity market cap. Missing half the world's stocks means missing diversification benefits and multi-year leadership cycles.
  • Audit single-stock concentration in your portfolioNo single stock should exceed 5% of your portfolio. Particularly dangerous: employer stock (doubles risk), highly concentrated inherited positions, or "hot" single stocks. Exceptions: if you are genuinely on the founder/CEO wealth-building road.
  • Stop relying on seasonal rules and calendar indicatorsIgnore: "Sell in May," "So goes January," "Don't fight the Fed," P/E-based timing, VIX signals, consumer confidence, oil price signals, and dollar direction. None reliably predict stock returns.
  • Create a "homegrown dividends" cash flow systemKeep 2-3 months of cash flow needs in portfolio cash at all times. Sell strategically (tax losses, trimming overweights) rather than chasing dividend yield. Be cash flow agnostic about the source.
  • Establish your holding period commitment in writingWrite down your minimum holding period for each investment before buying. For equity strategies, this should be 5-10+ years. Review this document every time you feel the urge to trade.
  • Create a "behavioral accountability" systemDesignate a trusted person (spouse, advisor, accountant) who must approve any major strategy change. Every time you want to deviate from your long-term plan, tell them — this friction prevents panic decisions.
  • Learn to read long-term stock charts on a log scaleAlways view 20+ year stock charts on a logarithmic scale. Linear-scale charts make recent gains look parabolic and scary — log scale shows consistent % growth. This eliminates fear of height in stock prices.
  • Stop worrying about gold, oil, GDP, and trade deficits as stock predictorsRun the correlations yourself in Excel. Oil vs stocks: -0.02. Dollar vs stocks: no pattern. GDP vs stocks: stocks lead GDP by 6-12 months. These are not investment signals.
  • Understand the presidential term cycle and set expectations accordinglyYears 1-2 of a presidency: more volatile, higher downside risk (political legislation risk). Years 3-4: historically more positive, less legislation. Year 3 has averaged +17.5% since 1939 with no negative years.
  • Internalize that unemployment rises after recessions end — and after stocks bottomNever wait for unemployment to fall before investing. Historically, the best 12-month returns come when you invest while unemployment is still rising. Stocks bottom 3-9 months before unemployment peaks.
  • Practice regular debunkery on your existing beliefsFor every investing belief you hold: (1) Scale it, (2) Check history, (3) Think globally, (4) Run a four-box analysis, (5) Look for counterexamples. Question what you think you know.
